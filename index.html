01 · Research

How do stem cells, genomes and cell types evolve?

Cnidarians (corals, jellyfish, sea anemones and hydroids) are the sister group to bilaterian animals, including us. Some regenerate their whole body, keep pluripotent stem cells throughout life and show no signs of ageing. I combine wet-lab experiments with genomics and data science to work out how they do it, and what that tells us about animal biology as a whole.

Stem cells & whole-body regeneration

Hydractinia symbiolongicarpus

Showed that adult i-cells are pluripotent and germ-cell competent, and that cellular senescence drives reprogramming during whole-body regeneration. Also showed that whole animals can rebuild themselves from dissociated, reaggregated cells (under review after revision).

Curr Biol 2023 · Cell Rep 2023 · bioRxiv 2025

Single-cell atlases & genomes

Hydractinia · Nematostella vectensis

Built the Hydractinia whole-animal cell type atlas using SPLiT-seq, which revealed the cellular and molecular principles of coloniality.

Co-first and corresponding author, Nature Communications 2025

Contributed to the chromosome-level Hydractinia genome. Led complete, chromosome-level Nematostella genome assemblies (manuscript in preparation).

Nat Commun 2025 · G3 2023 · Nvec genomes

Evolution of cell death & genome stability

Metazoa · Lophotrochozoa · Hydractinia

Traced how apoptotic signalling pathways diverged across animals, and characterised the DNA damage response of a non-ageing animal, including how its stem cells survive ionising radiation.

An independent collaboration, run without my PhD supervisor. I was first and corresponding author of the lophotrochozoan study (GBE 2024).
GBE 2024 · Evol Lett 2024 · Front 2022 · PhD 2024

Germline & sexual reproduction

Hydractinia

Helped show that a positive feedback loop between germ cells and gonads induces and maintains sexual reproduction in a cnidarian.

Sci Adv 2025

Lineage logic in Nematostella

Nematostella vectensis · Gibson Lab, Stowers

Building a spatio-temporal atlas of stem cell differentiation using spatial multiomics, transgenesis, conditional cell ablation and cell transplantation. The aim is to establish Nematostella as a research organism for stem cell biology and regeneration.

Stowers profile

Next: interspecies chimeras

Hydractinia × Podocoryna carnea

A Wellcome-funded, low-cost in vivo model of interspecies chimerism. It uses colony fusion, live imaging, flow cytometry and single-cell sequencing to find out how cells cross the species barrier.

Wellcome grant · incoming, University of Exeter

Single-cell genomics

Every cell, mapped

This is the real Hydractinia cell atlas: 195,300 cells, each placed by the genes it expresses (UMAP), with depth showing developmental pseudotime. Clusters are cell types, and the bridges trace stem cells (i-cells) as they become everything else.

I co-led the whole-animal Hydractinia cell atlas using SPLiT-seq, a low-cost split-pool barcoding method I helped bring to Ireland. That made single-cell genomics affordable for a non-traditional research organism. I'm now building spatio-temporal atlases of stem cell differentiation in Nematostella.

Data: Salamanca-Díaz, Horkan et al. 2025, Nat Commun. 7,000-cell subsample, colours adapted. Explore the full atlas on the UCSC Cell Browser →

Every -ome, for everyone

Flagship project · creator & lead developer

everyome

Every -ome, for everyone.

A free, open-source platform that gives any biologist a point-and-click interface to cutting-edge omics analysis, with every setting visible and editable. It runs on your own computer or your own HPC account, so your data stays where it is. It removes the cost and coding barriers that keep powerful methods out of many labs.

07 · Why basic science

Curiosity is infrastructure

Almost every medical breakthrough started as a question nobody could yet put a price on. CRISPR came from studying how bacteria fight viruses. Green fluorescent protein, now in labs everywhere, came from a jellyfish, a cnidarian. GLP-1 drugs trace back to the venom of a lizard. None of these discoveries could have been planned as products. They came from people funded to understand how life works.

Animals like Hydractinia and Nematostella have solved problems that we haven't: they regenerate their whole body, keep pluripotent stem cells for life and seem to avoid ageing and cancer. Understanding how they do it is an investment in knowledge that benefits everyone, and the returns are often ones nobody can predict.

Fund the questionsLong-term, curiosity-driven research is where unexpected breakthroughs come from. It needs stable public funding, not only money tied to expected applications.
Open by defaultPublicly funded knowledge should be publicly available: open-access papers, open data, open code and protocols anyone can follow.
Tools for allA discovery is only as useful as the number of people who can build on it. Free, approachable tools turn knowledge into progress everywhere, not only in well-funded labs.
Science for all of usThe progress of human knowledge belongs to everyone, and it moves faster when everyone can take part.

08 · About me

Where I come from, and why it matters

I grew up in Manchester, UK, in a low-income family. I'm a first-generation high school graduate, and I worked shift jobs all through my degree. A school physics teacher first showed me that science was something I could do. Even so, as a working-class woman, most of what I needed to know about academia I had to work out for myself.

That's why I care about access. Talent is everywhere; opportunity, money and insider knowledge are not. Every paywall, expensive kit or tool that needs years of coding experience shuts out people like the person I used to be. So I build free tools, write protocols anyone can follow, teach, and do outreach aimed at the people science usually misses, especially girls and students from working-class backgrounds.
